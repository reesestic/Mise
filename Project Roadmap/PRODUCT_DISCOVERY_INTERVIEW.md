# Mise Product Direction Interview

**Length:** 12–15 minutes  
**Use with:** People who buy or help buy groceries and decide what to cook  
**Goal:** Find out which food-planning problems are frequent and important, what people do today, and which product direction would help most. Do not try to sell Mise or get approval for a feature list.

## Interviewer notes

- Ask about the participant's own recent behavior, not what people in general might do.
- Ask the question as written before using the optional probes.
- Do not describe Mise until Section 4. Avoid naming features the participant has not mentioned.
- Ask what happened, what they did, and what the consequences were. “Would you use an app that…?” is not evidence of demand.
- Record direct quotes, examples, frequency, current workaround, and impact. Separate observed behavior from opinions or guesses.
- If the participant has no recent example, record that and move on; do not persuade them the problem exists.

## 0. Intro — 30 seconds

> I’m exploring how people decide what food to buy and cook. I’m interested in what you actually do, including things that work well. There are no right answers, and I’m not testing you.

## 1. Recent grocery shop — 3 minutes

1. **Think about your most recent grocery shop. What were you trying to get done?**
2. **How did you decide what to buy? Walk me through it.**
3. **What, if anything, was difficult, frustrating, or uncertain? What happened as a result?**

Optional probes, only if needed:

- How did you decide quantities or whether you already had enough at home?
- Did you have meals in mind, buy ingredients first, or use another approach?
- Did anything go unused, get forgotten, exceed your budget, or require another trip?
- How long did the shop take? Which decisions or steps took the most time or caused stress?
- How often does that happen? What did it cost you in time, money, or stress?

## 2. Recent cooking decision — 2 minutes

4. **Think about the last time you were at home and needed to decide what to eat. What did you do?**
5. **Did you have food at home that you could have used but didn’t think of, or did you struggle to find something appealing? Tell me about it.**
6. **How often does choosing what to cook feel like a problem? What do you do when it does?**

Optional probe: What makes you reject a meal idea—time, effort, cleanup, taste, missing ingredients, nutrition, equipment, or something else? Do you deliberately make extra servings for leftovers? What happens to them?

## 3. Current habits and priorities — 2 minutes

7. **How do you currently keep track of food at home, if at all?**
8. **What matters most when you shop or choose meals? Please rank your top three:**

- Spend less / stay within a budget
- Eat toward a nutrition or health goal
- Save time and effort (shopping, prep, cooking, cleanup)
- Use food already at home / avoid waste
- Have enough variety or try new foods
- Buy familiar foods the household will eat
- Convenience, availability, or store deals
- Other: ______

9. **Which of those has been hardest to achieve recently? Why?**

## 4. Compare possible kinds of help — 4 minutes

Read the options neutrally. Randomize their order across interviews if possible.

> Imagine a tool could focus on one of these jobs. Which would help you most in your real life, and which would help least? Why?

**A. Cook from what you own**  
Show meal ideas that can be made with food at home, plus ideas that need only a small number of extra ingredients.

**B. Explore food ideas**  
Help you browse, search, and save meals you might want to make later, whether or not you own the ingredients.

**C. Build a meal-informed shopping list**  
Suggest meals that fit your tastes and goals, combine their ingredients into one editable list, account for food already at home, and show what meals the list supports. Prices may be estimated unless reliable store data is available.

**D. Shop by ingredients or deals**  
Help you find affordable ingredients, use store deals, or assemble a flexible set of foods without starting from specific meals.

**E. Track food at home**  
Help you record what you own, what is running low or may expire, and what to restock.

10. **Which one would you choose first? Which would you choose last? What makes the difference?**
11. **Would you prefer to start shopping with meals you want to make, or with ingredients/budget and have meals suggested from there? Tell me about the last time you shopped in a way that fits.**
12. **If the prices were estimates rather than current store prices, how would that affect the usefulness of the meal-informed list? What would you need to see to trust it?**

Do not defend the concepts. Ask “Why?” or “What would you do instead?” once when the answer is vague.

## 5. Must-have versus nice-to-have — 2 minutes

13. **Of the jobs we discussed, which one would make the biggest practical difference for you in the next month? What would it help you do that you currently cannot do easily?**
14. **Which job sounds least useful or too much effort? Why?**
15. **What would need to happen for you to trust a tool’s meal or grocery suggestion enough to act on it?**

## How to interpret results

- Strong evidence is a recent, specific example plus meaningful frequency or impact and an imperfect workaround.
- Weak evidence is general enthusiasm, hypothetical willingness, or agreement after the interviewer describes the idea.
- Look for repeated behavior and tradeoffs across participants. One interview can reveal a hypothesis, not validate a market.
- If meal discovery ranks high but shopping help does not, consider a discovery-first product. If Pantry-aware cooking ranks high, focus Cook Now and inventory. If budgeted meal-to-list planning ranks high despite estimated prices, test the shared-basket experience. If current prices/deals dominate, treat retailer data access as a core dependency or reconsider that direction.
- Do not count feature requests as proof of a painful problem unless the participant can connect them to an actual situation.
